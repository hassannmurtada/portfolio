Current work

Running an autonomy stack on a vehicle that actually drives

Most autonomy work isn't inventing planners. It's making a stack that works in a paper behave itself on a real vehicle, on a real road, with a person sitting in it.

I work on an Autoware Universe stack deployed on a full-size vehicle, as an Embodied AI Autonomous Driving Engineering Intern. The platform has driven over 50 km autonomously across campus and road routes — measured as distance actually driven, not route length on a map.

My work sits at two ends of the stack. At the bottom, the vehicle interface: translating the planner's output into the low-level commands the vehicle's drive-by-wire layer accepts, and the autonomous emergency braking behaviour that runs on top of it. At the top, perception: I took the vehicle from LiDAR-only sensing to a working camera–LiDAR fusion pipeline, including the calibration it depends on.

In between is the part nobody writes about: diagnosing why the vehicle did something strange two hundred metres ago. Localisation drift, actuation latency, handoff between planner and controller. This is most of the job, and it's the part I've learned the most from.

Engineering note

Why fusing a camera with a LiDAR is a geometry problem, not a sensor problem

A LiDAR knows where things are but not what they are. A camera knows what things are but not where. Fusion exists to move information across that gap — and it only ever moves in one direction.

A LiDAR measures time of flight, so its range is direct and metric: a point at 12.4 m is at 12.4 m. It has no idea whether those points are a traffic cone or a fire hydrant. A camera has enormous angular detail — colour, texture, lettering — but a pixel only tells you the ray the light arrived along, not how far along it the object sits. That's scale ambiguity, and it's geometry, not a hardware limitation to engineer away.

So fusion asks one question, over and over: given a 3D point, which pixel does it land on? Answer that and you can read off what the camera saw there and attach it to the point. The reverse — recovering 3D from a pixel — is not available, because the projection divides by depth and destroys it.

Calibration is the whole game, and it fails silently

Everything above rests on ten numbers: six extrinsic, four intrinsic. Get them wrong and nothing crashes, nothing warns you — the projection simply lands in the wrong place, and the system confidently attaches the wrong label or drops the object entirely.

An angular error of θ displaces the projection by roughly d · tan θ at distance d. That gets bad faster than intuition suggests:

Order matters Intrinsics must be solved before extrinsics. Extrinsic calibration works by projecting points into the image and minimising alignment error — so if the intrinsics are wrong, the solver quietly finds a wrong mounting pose that happens to cancel the intrinsic error. It converges. It looks correct. It is meaningless.

Geometry isn't enough — the clocks have to agree too

At 5 m/s, a 100 ms timing mismatch means the vehicle moved half a metre between the LiDAR sweep and the camera frame. Everything projects 50 cm off — and it is indistinguishable from a calibration error in the output, while having a completely different cause. A spinning LiDAR makes this worse: points at the start and end of a single sweep are already a full rotation period apart from each other.

The upgrade that makes things worse

Fusion frameworks are written on the assumption of a full camera ring. Under that assumption, a sensible default says: if no camera saw it, it's probably noise — discard it. Inherit that default on a vehicle with one forward camera and a detector that has never seen your object class, and enabling fusion takes you from "detected, but unlabelled" to "not detected at all."

The lesson worth more than the parameter Defaults encode assumptions about hardware you may not have. Every default that says "if the camera didn't see it, drop it" becomes a self-inflicted blind spot when you have one camera instead of eight. Audit them rather than inheriting them — and reason about what a change will do before you make it, because the failure mode here is silent.

This note is written from general principles. It contains no configuration, parameters, hardware layout or source from my employer's vehicle.

Competition robot

A robot that sees a hoop, solves the physics, and takes the shot

Autonomous scoring, on a competition floor, against a clock. The interesting constraint wasn't accuracy — it was that everything had to happen inside a frame, on a small onboard GPU, and never hang.

I owned the software from the camera to the motor command: the detector that finds the basket, the projectile-motion solver that computes launch velocity and angle from the measured distance, the localisation, and the micro-ROS link down to the board. A teammate built the STM32 mainboard firmware; I built on top of it, against an interface we agreed on first.

  1. Solve the shot, don't tabulate it Rather than hard-coding launch power per position, the robot measures distance to the hoop and solves launch velocity and angle from projectile motion. It shoots from anywhere on the floor instead of from marked spots — and when it misses, the error is a physical parameter to correct, not a lookup table to re-tune.
  2. Always act on the newest frame The camera feed drops stale frames rather than queueing them. When you're aiming in real time, acting on an old frame is worse than skipping one — a backed-up queue turns latency into a control error that grows.
  3. Quantise for the hardware you actually have The trained detector was exported to TensorRT so inference fit the robot's small onboard GPU without starving the rest of the system. Model accuracy is irrelevant if it can't run in the loop.
  4. Fail to a safe pose, not to a bad reading When the hoop leaves the frame, the robot holds a centred aim rather than chasing noise. Degrading predictably beats degrading confidently.
  5. Agree the interface, then build in parallel Vision and firmware talked through one fixed message contract, so my teammate and I could develop and test independently without blocking each other. Most of what looks like an integration problem is really an interface that was never written down.
