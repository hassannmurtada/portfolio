Most autonomy work isn't inventing planners. It's taking a stack that works on paper and making it behave on real hardware.

My work sat at both ends of the stack. At the bottom was the vehicle interface: translating the planner's output into the low-level commands the vehicle's drive-by-wire layer accepts, plus the emergency braking behaviour that runs on top of it. At the top was perception: I took the vehicle from LiDAR-only sensing to a working camera–LiDAR fusion pipeline, including the calibration it depends on.

In between is the part nobody writes about: figuring out why the vehicle did something strange two hundred metres ago. Localisation drift, actuation latency, the handoff between planner and controller. That's most of the job, and it's where I learned the most.

Calibration appears inline in the diagram above, not as a footnote, because every stage after it depends on it without anyone noticing. See Lessons for why.

+Five rules this robot taught me · 1 min
  1. Solve the shot, don't tabulate it. The robot measures distance and solves projectile motion, so when it misses, the error is a physical parameter to fix, not a lookup table to re-tune.
  2. Always act on the newest frame. Stale camera frames are dropped, not queued. When you aim in real time, a queue that backs up adds lag, and the lag becomes aiming error.
  3. Optimise for the hardware you have. The detector was exported to TensorRT so it fit the small onboard GPU. An accurate model is useless if it can't keep up with the control loop.
  4. Fail to a safe pose, not a bad reading. If the hoop leaves the frame, the robot holds a centred aim instead of chasing noise. It's better to fail in a predictable way than to act confidently on bad data.
  5. Agree the interface, then build in parallel. One fixed message contract between vision and firmware meant neither of us blocked the other. Most "integration problems" turn out to be an interface nobody wrote down.

02 / Lessons

Tiny errors, big consequences

A LiDAR knows where things are but not what they are. A camera knows what but not where. Fusion glues them together, and the glue is a handful of calibration numbers. Try it: mount the camera slightly crooked and watch what happens to a pedestrian.

Three silent failures worth knowing

Nothing crashes and nothing warns you. The output just looks a little wrong, and here's why.

The calibration converged, looked perfect, and was wrong.
CauseExtrinsics were solved before intrinsics. The solver found a wrong mounting pose that cancelled out the lens error.
FixAlways solve intrinsics first. A solver that converges is not proof the answer is right.
Everything projects 50 cm off, but the calibration is fine.
CauseThe clocks disagree. At 5 m/s, a 100 ms timing gap means the car moved half a metre between the LiDAR sweep and the camera frame.
FixCheck timestamps before you blame geometry. The two problems look identical in the output.
Turning fusion on made the car detect less.
CauseA default built for a full camera ring: "no camera saw it, so drop it." With one forward camera, that becomes a blind spot.
FixDefaults encode assumptions about hardware you may not have. Audit them instead of inheriting them.
+Read the full engineering note: why fusion is a geometry problem · 3 min

A LiDAR measures time of flight, so its range is direct and metric. It has no idea whether those points are a traffic cone or a fire hydrant. A camera has huge angular detail, but a pixel only tells you the ray the light arrived along, not how far along it the object is.

So fusion asks one question, over and over: given a 3D point, which pixel does it land on? Answer that, and you can attach what the camera saw to the point. The reverse, recovering 3D from a pixel, isn't possible, because the projection divides by depth and throws it away.

Ten numbers, and they fail silently

Everything rests on six extrinsic and four intrinsic numbers. Get them wrong and the projection lands in the wrong place, so the system confidently attaches the wrong label or drops the object.
