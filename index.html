Autonomous Basketball Robot · ABU Robocon 2025

A competition robot that sees the hoop, aims itself, computes the physics of the shot, and scores — fully autonomously. I built the software brain end-to-end: the vision that finds the basket, the projectile-motion solver that works out how hard and at what angle to launch, the localization that tracks the robot, the micro-ROS link to the board, and the integration that made it all run in real time on a live competition floor.

// WHAT I BUILT
  • The camera and YOLO vision that finds the basket (optimized with TensorRT)
  • The shooting — working out the launch speed and angle from projectile motion
  • The flywheel and motor control that actually takes the shot
  • The localization that keeps track of where the robot is
  • The micro-ROS link carrying everything to the board
  • The cloud training (Vast.ai) and AWS dataset setup for the team
  • Putting it all together and testing it on the field

My teammate built the STM32 mainboard firmware and board bring-up; I built my pipeline on top of it.

// SIGNAL PIPELINE — camera to motors
Camera→ YOLO / TensorRT→ Projectile solver→ micro-ROS→ STM32F407→ Flywheel + Motors

My detector finds the basket each frame, estimates the distance, and centers the aim by driving the basket's horizontal offset to zero. From that distance my projectile-motion solver computes the launch velocity and angle the ball needs, and sends it over the micro-ROS link — benchmarked at ~230–450 Hz — to the mainboard, which spins the flywheel and takes the shot. // SOME THINGS I'M GLAD I GOT RIGHT
  • Rather than hard-coding how hard to shoot, I worked out the launch speed and angle from the distance to the hoop, so the robot adjusts its shot wherever it's standing instead of only working from one spot.
  • For the camera feed I made the robot always act on the newest frame rather than work through a backed-up queue — when you're aiming in real time, reacting to an old frame is worse than skipping one.
  • To keep detection fast on the robot's small onboard GPU, I exported the trained model to TensorRT so it could run quickly without choking the system.
  • If the camera loses sight of the basket, the robot doesn't panic — it just holds a centered aim instead of reacting to a bad reading.
  • I kept the vision and firmware talking through one simple, agreed-on message, so my teammate and I could each build and test our half without getting in each other's way.
